The **East India Company (EIC)** was a British trading company established in 1600, which eventually became a powerful political and military force in India. Its relationship with the **Princely States** (semi-autonomous kingdoms under British suzerainty) was complex and evolved over time. Below are detailed notes on the EIC and its relations with the Princely States:

---

### **1. Background of the East India Company**
- The EIC was granted a royal charter by Queen Elizabeth I in 1600 to trade in the East Indies.
- Initially focused on trade (spices, textiles, and later opium), the EIC gradually expanded its influence in India through diplomacy, alliances, and military conquests.
- By the mid-18th century, the EIC transitioned from a trading company to a territorial power, particularly after the **Battle of Plassey (1757)** and the **Battle of Buxar (1764)**.

---

### **2. Princely States in India**
- Princely States were semi-independent kingdoms ruled by local monarchs (Maharajas, Nawabs, Rajas, etc.).
- These states were not directly ruled by the British but were under British **suzerainty** (paramountcy), meaning they acknowledged British authority in exchange for internal autonomy.
- Examples of major Princely States: Hyderabad, Mysore, Jammu and Kashmir, Travancore, and Gwalior.

---

### **3. EIC's Early Relations with Princely States**
- **Alliances and Treaties**: The EIC formed alliances with Princely States to secure trade routes and protect its interests. For example:
  - The **Treaty of Allahabad (1765)** with the Mughal Emperor granted the EIC the right to collect revenue (diwani) in Bengal, Bihar, and Orissa.
  - The EIC signed treaties with states like Awadh and Hyderabad for military and financial support.
- **Subsidiary Alliance System**: Introduced by **Lord Wellesley (1798-1805)**, this system required Princely States to:
  - Accept British troops in their territory.
  - Pay for the maintenance of these troops.
  - Conduct foreign relations only through the British.
  - Examples: Hyderabad (1798) and Mysore (1799) were among the first to enter into such alliances.
  - **Treaty of Amritsar - In **1846**, a significant event involving the Princely States was the **Treaty of Amritsar** (March 16, 1846), which was signed between the **British East India Company** and **Gulab Singh**, the Dogra ruler of Jammu.
       Key provisions of the **Treaty of Amritsar (1846)**:
         - Gulab Singh was recognized as the Maharaja of Jammu and Kashmir.  
         - He paid the British **75 lakh rupees** (7.5 million) for the territory.
         - The treaty established the princely state of Jammu and Kashmir under British suzerainty.

---

### **4. EIC's Expansion and Control Over Princely States**
- **Doctrine of Lapse**: Introduced by **Lord Dalhousie (1848-1856)**, this policy allowed the EIC to annex Princely States if the ruler died without a natural heir.
  - Examples: Satara (1848), Jhansi (1853), and Nagpur (1854) were annexed under this policy.
- **Annexation of Awadh (1856)**: Awadh was annexed on the pretext of misgovernance, which angered many Indians and contributed to the **Revolt of 1857**.

---

### **5. Princely States During the Revolt of 1857**
- The Revolt of 1857 (First War of Indian Independence) saw mixed responses from Princely States:
  - Some rulers, like the **Rani of Jhansi** and **Nana Saheb**, actively supported the revolt.
  - Others, like the **Maharaja of Kashmir** and the **Nizam of Hyderabad**, remained loyal to the British.
- After the revolt, the British Crown took direct control of India from the EIC in 1858, and the relationship with Princely States was redefined.

---

### **6. Post-1857: Princely States Under British Crown**
- The **Government of India Act (1858)** abolished the EIC and established the British Crown's direct rule over India.
- Princely States retained internal autonomy but were firmly under British paramountcy.
- The British used the Princely States as a buffer zone and a source of revenue and military support.

---

### **7. Legacy of EIC's Relations with Princely States**
- The EIC's policies laid the foundation for the British colonial administration in India.
- The Princely States became a key part of the British strategy to "divide and rule."
- After India's independence in 1947, the Princely States were integrated into the Indian Union, largely through the efforts of **Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel**.

---

### **8. Key Takeaways**
- The EIC's relationship with Princely States evolved from trade and alliances to political control and annexation.
- Policies like the Subsidiary Alliance and Doctrine of Lapse weakened the Princely States and consolidated British power.
- The Princely States played a significant role in India's political landscape until independence.
